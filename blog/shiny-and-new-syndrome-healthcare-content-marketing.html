Shiny and New Syndrome: Why Healthcare Content Marketing Fails

Messick Marketing October 2026 6 min read

A specialty practice builds out a solid content strategy. Good messaging, clear positioning, a consistent posting cadence. Everyone is on board. Then, somewhere around week six or eight, something new comes along. A new platform, a new trend, a new format everyone is talking about. And they drop everything to chase it.

I see this constantly, and it is quietly killing results for practices that did almost everything right. I call it shiny and new syndrome.

Hi, I'm Morgan with Messick Marketing. The video below is the short version. This post is the longer one: why the syndrome shows up right when it does, how long a content strategy really needs before you can judge it, how to tell the difference between "needs more time" and "needs a real change," and what to do with the new shiny thing instead of pulling up your garden for it.

What is shiny and new syndrome?

Shiny and new syndrome is the habit of abandoning a working content strategy, before it has had time to work, for whatever is newest. The new thing is rarely bad. It is just new, and new feels like progress in a way that week seven of the same plan never does.

It is one of the single biggest reasons content marketing fails for healthcare practices. Not because the strategy was wrong, but because it never got the time it needed. Most of the strategies I see get scrapped would have worked. They were killed in the dip, not at the finish line.

Why does it always hit around week six to eight?

The timing is not a coincidence. The first few weeks of a content strategy run on momentum. The plan is fresh, the team is excited, the first posts go up and a few people say nice things. Then the newness wears off and the numbers have not moved yet, because they are not supposed to have moved yet.

That is the dip, and the dip is where the shiny thing finds you. A colleague mentions a platform. A consultant posts about a format that is "changing everything." A practice across town is doing something you are not. Each one looks like a shortcut out of the dip, and the strategy you built looks like the thing holding you there.

Healthcare practices feel this harder than most businesses, because the people making the call are busy clinicians and managers. Content is a side project for them. When a side project is not showing results by week eight, the natural move is to replace it with a different side project, not to sit with it.

"You do not put seeds in the ground on Tuesday and expect flowers by Friday."

Why does content need time to take root?

Think about it like planting a garden. You do not put seeds in the ground on Tuesday, water once, and expect flowers by Friday. You water it daily. You tend to it. You give it the care and the time it needs to take root before you ever see what it is capable of producing.

Content works exactly the same way, and there are three specific things that need that time:

  • The algorithm needs time to learn you. Every platform is deciding who to show your posts to, and it decides from a track record. A new account with eight posts has barely given it anything to work with. Thirty consistent posts on one clear topic teach it who you are for.
  • Your audience needs time to build trust with you. A patient does not book after one post. She sees you, scrolls past, sees you again, reads one, saves one, and six weeks later her sister mentions a symptom and your name is the one she already knows. Referring providers work the same way, only slower.
  • Your content compounds on itself. Post twelve gets found because post four is still being searched. A blog post from last spring sends someone to a video from this month. None of that happens when the body of work keeps starting over.

Pull up the seeds every time something else catches your eye and you reset all three clocks at once. The platform starts learning you from zero, your audience sees a practice that keeps changing its mind, and nothing compounds because nothing stays.

How long should you give a content strategy before judging it?

Longer than feels comfortable. For a specialty practice posting consistently, I want at least 90 days before anyone draws a conclusion, and I would rather see six months. The first real signs, like saves, shares, profile visits and the occasional "I saw your video," usually show up somewhere in month two or three. Referral relationships and steady new-patient inquiries from content are a month six to twelve story.

That is not a reason to post blindly for a year. It is a reason to decide, up front, what you will measure and when, so that week seven does not become a referendum on the whole plan. If you have not written that down, the content strategy guide walks through it.

How do you tell "needs more time" from "needs a real change"?

This is the question practices actually want answered, because sometimes the strategy really is the problem. Here is the test I use.

It probably needs more time if:

  • You are posting on the cadence you planned, and have been for fewer than 90 days.
  • The content is specific to the patients and conditions you actually want more of.
  • Small signals are moving: saves, shares, a few profile visits, a staff member hearing "I saw your post."
  • The reason you want to change is that something new appeared, not that something measurable went wrong.

It probably needs a real change if:

  • Nobody is actually posting on schedule. A strategy that is not being executed cannot be judged. Fix the execution first.
  • The content could belong to any clinic in your city. Generic posts do not compound, because there is nothing for the algorithm or a patient to attach to you.
  • Three months in, the only engagement is from staff and family.
  • You are getting attention from the wrong people, which is the going viral problem, and not from the patients or referrers you built the plan for.

Notice that the second list is about the content and the execution. The first list is about the calendar. Shiny and new syndrome is what happens when a calendar problem gets treated like a content problem.

What should you do when something new comes along?

You do not have to ignore it. New platforms and formats are sometimes worth a look. What you cannot do is let the new thing replace the plan. So give it a lane.

  1. Keep the core running untouched. Your cadence, your pillars, your platforms. That is the garden, and it does not get dug up.
  2. Test the new thing on the side, small. One format for four weeks, or one new platform fed with content you already made for the core. If it needs its own strategy and its own production, it is not a test, it is a second plan, and you are not ready for a second plan.
  3. Decide in advance what "it worked" means. A number, a date. Otherwise the test becomes the next shiny thing's victim too.
  4. Fold it in or let it go. If it earns a place, it becomes part of the core and gets the same patience. If not, you lost four weeks of a side lane, not six months of trust.

The practices I see get the best results are not the ones doing the most things on the most platforms. They are the ones doing the right things consistently and giving their strategy room to breathe.

Common questions about shiny and new syndrome

What is shiny and new syndrome in content marketing?

It is the habit of abandoning a working content strategy for whatever platform, trend or format is newest, usually six to eight weeks in, before the original plan has had time to produce results.

How long does healthcare content marketing take to work?

Give a consistent strategy at least 90 days before judging it, and expect referral relationships and steady patient inquiries to take six to twelve months. Early signals like saves, shares and profile visits usually appear in month two or three.

Why does my practice's content feel like it is going nowhere?

Nine times out of ten the strategy is not the problem, the patience is. The algorithm, your audience and your body of work all need time, and switching plans resets all three.

Should a medical practice try new social media platforms?

Sometimes, but as a small side test with a set end date, fed with content you already make. The core strategy keeps running untouched while you decide whether the new platform earns a place.

How do I know if my content strategy actually needs to change?

Change it if nobody is executing the schedule, the posts could belong to any clinic, or after three months the only engagement is from staff and family. If you are posting consistently and the only thing that changed is something new appeared, give it more time.

Stop pulling up the seeds

Your strategy was probably fine. What it did not get was time. The algorithm needs time to learn you, your audience needs time to trust you, and your content needs time to compound, and every shiny new thing you chase resets all three.
