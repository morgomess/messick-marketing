Ads Rent Attention. Content Earns Trust.

Messick Marketing October 2026 6 min read

An interventional radiology practice runs a month of ads for uterine fibroid embolization. The calls come in. Then the budget ends, and so do the calls. Not slowly. The week the ads stop, the phone goes quiet.

Nothing went wrong. That is simply what an ad is. You paid for attention, you got it, and when the payments stopped, the attention went back to whoever was paying next.

Content works on a different clock. A clear explanation of what fibroid embolization involves, written for a woman who was just told a hysterectomy is her only option, keeps answering that question for months. Nobody has to keep feeding it a budget.

What does an ad actually buy?

A moment of attention, rented, from whoever matches the targeting. That is a real thing, and sometimes it is exactly what a practice needs. But it is worth being honest about what is in the box.

An ad does not explain your treatment. It does not answer the question a patient is too embarrassed to ask at the front desk. It does not show them who you are before they walk in. It gets a few seconds, and then it is gone.

Healthcare makes that rental more expensive. The major ad platforms limit how precisely you can target people based on health conditions, for good privacy reasons. So the ad reaches a broad audience, and the practice pays for every person in it, including the many who will never need what it offers.

"Ads rent attention. Content earns trust."

Why do specialty practices feel this the most?

Because the pool is small, and most of the people in it do not know they belong there.

Think about a pelvic floor physical therapy practice. The patients who would benefit most are often living with symptoms they assume are just part of having had children, or of getting older. They are not searching for "pelvic floor PT near me." They do not know it exists. An ad aimed at people searching for it will never reach them.

Or a behavioral health practice that offers ADHD evaluations for adults. Plenty of adults have spent years wondering why focus is so hard for them. Far fewer have ever typed "adult ADHD assessment" into a search bar. They are reading articles about procrastination and burnout, trying to figure out what is going on.

Ads reach people who already know what they want. Specialty practices mostly need to reach the people who do not know yet. That is a content job.

What does content earn that ads can't?

Four things, and they compound.

  • Answers before the call. Patients research for weeks before they book. Content that answers their real questions, in plain language, is what they find while they are deciding.
  • Trust before the first appointment. A patient who has read your explanation, watched your provider talk through the procedure, and seen how you answer common worries walks in already halfway to yes.
  • Work that keeps working. A blog post from last year can still rank. A short video from three months ago can still pick up views. Every piece you publish adds to the pile instead of disappearing when the invoice is paid.
  • A place in AI answers. More patients now ask an AI tool about their symptoms before they search for a provider. Those tools build their answers from published content. An ad is not something they can quote.

None of that is about volume. It is about being the clearest, most trustworthy answer for the specific patients your practice was built to treat.

So should a practice stop running ads?

No. This is not an argument against ads. It is an argument about what goes first.

Ads work best when they amplify content that is already working. Retarget the people who read your post about knee replacement recovery and are clearly weighing their options. Put a little budget behind the short video that patients keep sharing. That is renting attention on top of trust you already earned, and it is a far better use of the money.

What does not work is renting attention with nothing behind it. If the ad sends someone to a thin service page with no answers, the click is the whole relationship. They leave, and the budget is spent.

Where should a practice start?

Smaller than you think. You do not need a hundred posts. You need the right few.

  1. Write down the three questions patients ask most. Ask your front desk. Ask your providers what they explain over and over. Those are your first topics.
  2. Make one blog post and one short video for each. The post answers it fully for the patient who reads. The video answers it in under a minute for the patient who scrolls.
  3. Measure the right thing. Not raw views. Watch whether the patients calling you are the ones you are built to treat, and whether they arrive already understanding their options.

Then keep going, a few pieces a month, every month. That consistency is the whole advantage. Your competitors' ads stop when their budget does. Your content does not.

Common questions about ads and content for healthcare practices

Is content marketing slower than ads?

At the start, yes. An ad can produce calls this week, while content usually takes a few months to build momentum. The difference is what happens after. Ad results end when spending ends. Content results keep adding up, so over a year the comparison usually looks very different.

Can a small specialty practice compete with bigger groups on content?

Often better than on ads. Bigger groups can outspend you on ads. They rarely out-explain you. A small practice that answers its patients' real questions clearly and consistently can become the most trusted voice for its specialty in its area.

What kind of content builds the most trust with patients?

Content that answers a real question in plain language, ideally from the people who will actually treat them. Short videos of providers explaining a procedure, honest posts about recovery, and clear answers to common worries tend to do the most work.

How do we know if our content is reaching the right patients?

Look past total views. Ask new patients how they found you and what they read or watched first. Watch which posts bring in consultation requests, and whether those patients are a good fit for what you offer. That tells you far more than a reach number ever will.
