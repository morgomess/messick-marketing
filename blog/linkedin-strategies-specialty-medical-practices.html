LinkedIn for Specialty Practices: What to Post and Why (and Who Is Actually Reading)

Messick Marketing November 2026 6 min read

LinkedIn Is Not Instagram. That Is the Point.

Most specialty practices have a LinkedIn page. Most of them have not posted since someone set it up. Meanwhile, the practice's Instagram gets a reel every week and the Facebook page gets a holiday graphic every month. The energy goes where the patients are, and that logic makes sense until you realize that LinkedIn is where the referrers are.

This is the channel where a primary care physician scrolls during a lunch break, where a nurse practitioner looks up a specialist before sending a patient, and where a practice administrator evaluates a potential hire. The audience is not the patient. The content that works here looks nothing like what performs on your other channels.

Who Is Actually Reading Your LinkedIn Content

Before writing a single post, name the reader. For a urology group whose new patients arrive mostly through primary care referrals, the reader is a family medicine physician or internal medicine NP deciding whether they trust the group enough to send a patient. For a pelvic floor therapy program seeking OB/GYN and urogynecology referrals, the reader is a clinician who needs to know the program exists, what it treats, and how to send someone. For a pain management practice, the reader is an orthopedic surgeon or primary care provider who needs confidence that the practice handles complex cases and communicates back promptly. For an audiology practice co-managing patients with local ENT physicians, the reader is the ENT coordinator looking at whether the relationship is worth formalizing.

Potential hires read too. So do hospital credentialing staff, group purchasing contacts, and local business leaders who influence your reputation in ways that do not show up in a referral report.

Write for all of them, and write nothing that reads like a patient-facing ad.

What the Practice Page Should Post

Think of the practice page as the front desk of your professional reputation. It answers the questions a referrer has before they ever pick up the phone. Good practice page content does three things: it confirms who you see, it explains how the referral process works, and it signals that the team is stable and competent.

Concretely, that looks like this. A urology group posts a short piece on what a new referral needs to include and how fast the group returns communication to the referring office. A pelvic floor therapy program posts a provider spotlight on its clinical director, with a sentence on the evaluations she conducts and the conditions she works best with. A pain management practice posts an update when it adds a new service or a new provider, written not for the patient but for the orthopedic office that now has one more option to offer.

What belongs on the practice page:

  • New provider announcements, written to introduce the clinician to colleagues
  • Referral logistics updates, such as changes to scheduling, fax lines, or intake forms
  • Practice milestones that signal stability, an accreditation, a new location, a partnership
  • Short educational posts that help a referrer explain a referral to a patient
  • Hiring posts, written to attract clinical and administrative talent

What does not belong:

  • Patient stories, even anonymized ones, without documented written consent
  • Posts that read like paid ads
  • Clinical outcome claims
  • Anything you would not say at a hospital grand rounds

A realistic cadence for most practices is two posts per week. That is enough to stay visible in a referrer's feed without overwhelming a team that has other priorities.

What the Physician Should Post Personally

Here is a consistent truth about LinkedIn: people follow people more readily than they follow pages. A physician's personal profile almost always outperforms the practice page in reach, because the algorithm favors individual voices, and because a human being is more interesting to follow than a logo.

The physician's profile is not a resume and it is not a press release. Think of it as a curbside consult that happens before the formal referral. When an audiology practice's lead audiologist posts a short reflection on how co-management with ENT changes outcomes for a certain patient type, the ENT reading it gets something useful. That is the transaction. It is not promotional. It is collegial.

What works for a physician posting personally:

  • One short clinical lesson per week, case-free, that answers a question a referrer actually has
  • A reaction to a guideline change or a new clinical development in the field
  • A behind-the-scenes look at how the team works, what happens between the referral and the first appointment
  • Acknowledgment of a colleague or a staff member, written with specificity

None of this requires the physician to become a content creator. It requires fifteen minutes once a month.

Getting a Reluctant Physician to Post

Most physicians will not write LinkedIn posts on their own, and asking them to is usually where the strategy stalls. The fix is simple. Sit down with the physician for fifteen minutes once a month. Ask four questions: What is a question you answered for a referrer this week? What changed in your field recently that more people should know about? What does your team do that surprises people? What do you wish the clinicians sending you patients understood better?

Take the answers, clean them up, and turn them into four posts. Send them to the physician for a quick approval read. Post one per week. The physician's voice stays authentic because the content is literally their words. The marketing lead's job is editing, not inventing.

This is not ghostwriting in the uncomfortable sense. It is the same process a journalist uses when turning an interview into an article. The physician said it. You shaped it. They approved it. That is a reasonable division of labor.

Cadence, Compliance, and Knowing What Is Working

A manageable cadence for most specialty practices is two practice page posts and one physician post per week. That is three posts total, spread across two channels, and it is enough to maintain visibility with a referral audience that is not expecting daily content from a medical practice.

On compliance: nothing on LinkedIn replaces a formal HIPAA review of your social media policy. Do not post patient information, do not post images taken in clinical settings without documented consent, and do not make claims about treatment outcomes. When in doubt, ask your compliance officer before you post.

On knowing what is working: LinkedIn's native analytics show follower growth, post impressions, and engagement by job title. That last data point matters. If your urology group's posts are reaching primary care physicians and internists, the content is finding the right room. If it is reaching patients and job seekers but not referrers, the content needs to shift toward the clinical and operational topics that speak to a provider audience.

The LinkedIn Page You Have Is Not the LinkedIn Page You Need

A page that has not been touched in a year is not neutral. It signals to a referrer checking your credentials that the practice is not paying attention to this channel, and by extension, may not be paying attention to the relationship. The fix is not complicated. It is consistent, deliberate, and aimed at the right reader.

Start with the practice page. Add a post this week that answers the most common question your front desk gets from a referring office. Then find fifteen minutes with one physician and ask the four questions. The strategy begins there.
