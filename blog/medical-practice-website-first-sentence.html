Your Website Is Not the Problem, Your First Sentence Is

Messick Marketing October 2026 6 min read

A practice owner tells me their website is old, nobody ever calls from it, and they need a new one. So I pull up the site. Right at the top, in giant block letters, it says: "Welcome to our practice. We are committed to excellence in patient care."

That one sentence is the entire problem. Not the branding, not the colors, not the layout. That sentence. And a brand new website with the same sentence at the top is going to have the exact same problem.

Hi, I'm Morgan with Messick Marketing. In the video below I walk through why this happens and rewrite a sample page live. This post goes deeper on the part you can act on today: what a patient is looking for in the first few seconds, the three sentences that answer her, and how to tell when the problem really is the website.

What does a patient decide in the first four seconds?

Picture your ideal patient. She is on her phone, maybe in a waiting room, maybe lying in bed at night. She types in a symptom or a question about her condition, your practice shows up, and she taps your link.

From there she gives you about four seconds. She is not really reading yet. She is asking herself three things:

  1. Is this for me?
  2. Can they help with what I have?
  3. What do I do next?

If the top of your page answers those three questions right away, she keeps scrolling. If it does not, she taps back and moves on to the next result. She never tells you why. All you see on your end is a four second visit and zero inquiries.

Why does "committed to excellence" get skipped?

Run that original sentence through her three questions. Does "Welcome to our practice. We are committed to excellence in patient care." tell her the practice is for her? No. Does it tell her what you treat or how you help? No. Does it tell her how to get in touch? No.

Healthcare has an extra layer of difficulty here, because every other clinic in your city uses the same words. Committed to excellence. Compassionate care. State of the art. Patient centered approach. Those things may all be true about your practice. The trouble is that everyone says them, so they carry no meaning, and language with no meaning is exactly what a scanning reader skips.

"It was written so that nobody would object, not so that the right person would call."

This language survives because it feels safe. Nobody rejects it in an approval meeting, and there is no back and forth with the team. That is the issue. A sentence designed to offend no one is rarely the sentence that makes the right patient pick up the phone.

What are the three sentences every practice needs at the top of its site?

The fix is a simple three sentence structure. In short: who it is for, what you do, and what to do next.

Sentence one: who it is for and what is wrong

Use your patient's own words, not your procedure name. "Heavy legs." "Pain that wakes you up at night." "Newly diagnosed." She searched for a feeling, not a CPT code, so the first line should sound like the thing she typed.

Sentence two: what you do, specifically

This is where the details go. Where does the treatment happen? How long does it take? What does recovery look like? Be specific, because specific is what reads as believable. "Exceptional care" cannot be checked. "You go home the same day" can.

Sentence three: the one next step

Pick one action. Call, or book a consult. Not five options, not five links. One clear call to action she can take from her phone without thinking about it.

What does the rewrite look like on a real page?

In the video I rewrite a made up example, Riverside Vein Center. It is fictional, but it is very close to what I see on real practice sites every week. The original reads:

"Welcome to Riverside Vein Center. Our board-certified physicians are dedicated to providing exceptional, patient-centered vein care using the latest techniques."

What did you learn? That they have doctors and the doctors are good. Every clinic says that. Here is the same page through the three sentence framework:

  1. Who it is for: "Legs that ache, swell or feel heavy by the end of the day?"
  2. What you do: "We find the vein that is causing the problem and treat it right here in the office. Every patient goes home the same day and can return to work right away."
  3. What to do next: "Call to book a vein screening, and we will verify your insurance before you come in."

Same doctors, same website, completely different message. Notice what did not change: the design, the photos, the layout. The only thing that moved is what she sees in her first four seconds.

When is the website actually the problem?

Sometimes it really is the site. Fix these first:

  • It does not work well on a phone.
  • Pages load slowly.
  • The phone number is not tappable.
  • The design looks dated enough to cost you trust before anyone reads a word.

But if your site loads fast, works on mobile, and still nobody calls, look at your first sentence before you sign a contract for a rebuild. Common questions about homepage copy for medical practices

Why is my medical practice website not getting calls?

Often the design is fine and the first sentence is the problem. If the top of the page does not tell a patient who it is for, what you treat and what to do next within a few seconds, she leaves before she ever sees the rest of the site.

How long do patients spend deciding whether to stay on a practice website?

About four seconds. In that time a patient is not reading closely. She is checking whether the page is for her, whether you can help with her problem, and what her next step is.

What should the headline on a medical practice homepage say?

Use three sentences: who it is for and what is wrong in the patient's own words, what you do in specific terms, and one clear next step such as calling or booking a consult.

Why should practices avoid phrases like "committed to excellence"?

Because nearly every clinic uses them, so they do not set you apart and patients skip them. Specific details, like same day treatment or insurance verified before the visit, are more believable and more useful.

Do I need a new website or just new copy?

If your site is slow, hard to use on a phone, has a number that cannot be tapped, or looks badly dated, fix those first. If it loads fast, works on mobile and still does not produce calls, rewrite the copy at the top before paying for a rebuild.

Cut the line about excellence

Your patient gives you about four seconds. To keep her, you need three sentences that answer her three questions: who it is for, what you do, and what to do next. Everything else on the page only gets read if those three land.
