How to Optimize Your Website for AI Search (Using the SOAP Note)

Morgan Messick August 2026 8 min read

It is eleven at night. A woman with fibroids has her phone in her hand, and she is finally typing the question she has not yet worked up the nerve to ask her doctor. Are there any alternatives to a hysterectomy?

She is not typing it into Google. She is typing it into ChatGPT.

The answer comes back fully formed and it names three practices in her general area that do non-surgical fibroid treatment. There is a fourth practice about ten minutes from her house that specializes in exactly this. It is not in the answer.

That practice is not worse at the procedure. Its website was written to be read like a brochure, and the answer was assembled by a speed reader.

Hi, I'm Morgan. I run Messick Marketing, a content marketing agency helping specialized healthcare providers find and connect with their audience online. The fix is a four-step framework named after a note format you already write every day: start with the question your patient asked, open with the answer, arrange it so a machine can read it, and prove who you are. Rebuilding practice websites so answer engines can read them is most of what we do.

I walked through the fix in the video below. The rest of this post covers the same ground in writing, with the before-and-after rewrites the video did not have room for.

How does AI decide which practices to name?

The mechanism behind that answer is what should change how you write, so it is worth being precise about it.

An answer engine does not land on your homepage, read top to bottom, and form an impression of your practice. It pulls paragraphs. It lifts a passage from your site, a passage from a hospital system, a passage from a health publisher, and it aggregates them into a single answer that reads as though one knowledgeable source wrote the whole thing.

A paragraph from your website therefore gets extracted alone. Judged alone. Used alone, sitting next to text you did not write, under a question you did not choose.

"Every paragraph on your site has to survive being read by itself, because by itself is the only way AI will ever read it."

Most medical websites fail that test on the very first paragraph. Not because the writing is bad. Because it was built to be read in order, by a person who had already decided to be there.

What framework makes a website easy for AI to read?

Here is the part that makes this easy. You already know how to write a document whose sections stand alone, get scanned instead of read, and have to serve a reader who was not in the room. You write one after every patient encounter.

A SOAP note works because a physician who has never met the patient can open it, land anywhere in it, and get what they need. Subjective, objective, assessment, plan. Each section answers one thing, in a predictable place, in a form the next reader can lift without reading the rest.

An answer engine needs exactly that discipline, every section written to work for a reader who lands on it cold. So I teach AEO with the four letters of the SOAP note, pointed at a different reader:

  • Start with the question your patient actually asked.
  • Open with the answer, then give the context.
  • Arrange it so a machine can read it.
  • Prove who you are.

S: Start with the question your patient actually asked

Your site almost certainly has a page titled Uterine Fibroid Embolization. Outside of your building, close to nobody types that.

Patients type: does UFE work. Are fibroids painful. Is a hysterectomy my only option. Will I still be able to have children.

The page title is a service line. The search is a worry. Answer engines match the worry, so start there and let the clinical term arrive inside the answer, where it belongs.

The good news is that this step needs almost no research, because you answer these exact questions ten or fifteen times a day in the exam room. The largest marketing asset most practices own is the knowledge sitting in the physician's head that nobody has taken the time to write down.

Practical version: think back through your last twenty new-patient consults and write down the questions you answered out loud. That list is your content calendar.

O: Open with the answer, then give the context

Most practices do this backwards, and it is the single most common thing we fix.

Here is the shape of a typical paragraph on a physical therapy site:

Physical therapy is beneficial for a wide variety of patients. It can support recovery following an injury or a workplace incident, and it can also help you build strength and mobility over time.

Nothing in that paragraph is factually wrong, and it is still unusable to an answer engine. The patient asked whether PT helps plantar fasciitis, and the text takes four clauses to get anywhere near a yes.

Here is the same content, rewritten to lead with the answer:

Yes, physical therapy helps plantar fasciitis. Most plans combine stretching, gait work, and a gradual return to load, with your therapist setting the timeline after an assessment. Here is what a typical course of treatment looks like.

Answer first, context second, always. The nuance and the caveats do not disappear. They just stop standing in front of the door.

Answering first is the step that makes physicians nervous, so let me name the fear directly: does giving away the answer stop the patient from calling? No. Publishing a clear answer works the way a good curbside consult works. Being the one who answered clearly is what makes you the one they call.

A: Arrange it so a machine can read it

There is a one-question test for this, and you can run it on your own site in about ten minutes.

Take a paragraph off your website at random, drop it into a conversation it was not written for, and ask whether it still makes sense.

A paragraph that still makes sense on its own is already doing its job. It carries its own subject, its own claim, and enough context to be understood by someone who never read the sentence above it.

A paragraph that falls apart out of context is where the work is. The culprit is usually one of four things:

  • It opens with "it" or "this" and borrows all of its meaning from the heading above it.
  • It runs two hundred words and holds three separate ideas, so there is no clean piece to lift.
  • It lists services without making a claim about any of them.
  • It is written to a reader who already knows which page they are standing on.

Think of it the way you think about labeling a specimen. The lab never sees the room it came from. If the label does not carry the context, the sample is unusable no matter how good it is.

Question-shaped headings, short paragraphs, a real FAQ section, and structured data on the page all serve this same goal. None of it is glamorous. All of it is the difference between content that gets parsed and content that gets skipped.

P: Prove who you are

AI already knows what fibroid embolization is. It does not need your definition and it will never cite you for one.

What it does not know is that your practice has done hundreds of them, that your interventional radiologist is board certified and fellowship trained, that you have two locations, and that you take the cases other groups refer out. That is the part only you can supply, and it is the part that decides whether you get named.

Put that proof high on the page, and put it in words rather than in a logo. Names, credentials, case volume, subspecialty focus, the conditions you treat and the cities you treat them in. A procedure page with no practice visible on it reads to these systems the way a referral packet with no letterhead reads to you. Probably fine, impossible to act on.

One caution worth stating plainly. Claim only what is true and current, and keep any volume figure in a form you could defend out loud. An invented number is a liability long before it is a marketing problem.

Where should you start?

One page, not fifty. Pick it deliberately and work it end to end, because a single page done properly teaches you more about how your site reads to a machine than a site-wide audit will.

1

Pick the page that matters most

Not the homepage. The procedure you want more of, or the one you wish the phone rang about. One page, chosen because the outcome would actually change your month.

2

Write down five questions in the patient's words

The ones you answer out loud in clinic, phrased the way they get asked, not the way they get charted. Those become your headings.

3

Rewrite each section answer-first

Direct answer in the opening sentence, one idea per paragraph, context underneath. If a paragraph is holding two ideas, split it.

4

Add the proof, then re-run the test

Physician names, credentials, volume, locations, near the top. Then pull three paragraphs at random and check that each one still makes sense standing on its own.

Work the list in order, then do the next page. Momentum on this comes from finishing pages, not from auditing all of them.

Common questions about the SOAP framework

What is the SOAP framework for AEO?

The SOAP framework for AEO is a four-step method for structuring a healthcare website so AI tools can extract and cite it: start with the question your patient actually asked, open with the answer before the context, arrange the content so each paragraph stands alone, and prove who you are with names, credentials, and volume. It borrows its shape from the SOAP note because the discipline is the same, writing for a reader who lands anywhere and needs that section to work by itself.

Does AEO replace SEO?

No. SEO gets you ranked and AEO gets you cited, and the smartest practices are optimizing both in tandem. They share a foundation, because answer engines read the same web that search engines index, so a site that is fast, crawlable, and technically sound is the starting point for both.

Does answering the question first hurt my SEO?

No. Leading with a direct answer helps both. Search engines reward pages that satisfy the query quickly, and answer engines can only cite what they can cleanly extract. The same rewrite pays out twice.

How do I know whether a paragraph is ready for AI search?

Drop it into a conversation it was not written for and see whether it still makes sense. If it needs the heading above it, the sentence before it, or the page it lives on in order to mean anything, it is not ready. Rewrite it so it carries its own subject and its own claim.

Do I have to rewrite my whole website?

No, and you should not try. Most practices already have the material, written like a brochure instead of an answer. Restructuring your highest-value procedure page is faster and works better than starting over, and it shows you what the rest of the site needs.

How long before AI search picks up the changes?

Anyone handing you a specific number is guessing, because it is genuinely too early for clean benchmarks. Answer engines skim, read, and reread the web constantly as new questions come in, which is a faster loop than a traditional ranking climb. Think in months rather than weeks or years, and treat starting now as the leverage.

Back to eleven at night

She is going to get an answer either way. That is the part worth sitting with. The answer gets assembled whether or not your practice is in it, out of whatever paragraphs happened to be clean enough to lift.

The practice ten minutes from her house is not losing to a bigger marketing budget. It is losing to four paragraphs that were written to be read in order.

Fix those four paragraphs and you are in the answer. That is honestly the whole job, repeated across the pages that matter most.
